\title[Inequality measurement with machine-learning poverty maps]{Predicted welfare is too equal: inequality and poverty measurement with machine-learning poverty maps}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Quang-Vinh} \sur{Dang}}\email{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{British University Vietnam}, \orgaddress{\city{Hung Yen}, \country{Vietnam}}}

\abstract{%
Poverty maps built with machine learning from satellite imagery and other non-traditional data are increasingly used to measure inequality as well as to target transfers. This paper shows that such maps understate inequality by construction, up to estimation noise. A map reports conditional-mean predictions of welfare, and true welfare is a mean-preserving spread of its conditional mean. Every Lorenz-consistent inequality index, and every Foster--Greer--Thorbecke poverty measure with $\alpha\geq 1$ at a fixed line, computed on the conditional mean is therefore a lower bound on the population value. For the mean log deviation and the Theil index, the gap equals expected within-cell inequality, which links poverty-map inequality to ex-ante inequality of opportunity. Linear recalibration for shrinkage cannot close the gap. We evaluate corrections that simulate welfare from estimated conditional distributions on two real datasets and on simulated data. In a design-based test using 1.3 million households from the American Community Survey, plug-in maps understate the national Gini by 21\% with household predictors and 63\% with area-level predictors, while a residual bootstrap within bins of the prediction is within 2\%. In satellite poverty maps of 23 African countries, plug-in predictions understate between-cluster inequality by 11--18\%, and corrections that pool residuals across countries overstate it. Simulations with a known truth confirm the theory and the mechanisms. Plug-in poverty maps should be treated as lower bounds for inequality.
}

\keywords{Inequality measurement, Poverty mapping, Machine learning, Satellite data, Lorenz dominance, Inequality of opportunity}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Poverty maps built from satellite imagery, mobile phone records and other ``big data'' sources now cover almost every low- and middle-income country at a resolution of a few square kilometres \citep{jean2016,blumenstock2015,yeh2020,chi2022}. They are used to allocate cash transfers \citep{aiken2022,smythe2022}, to evaluate programmes \citep{ratledge2022}, and increasingly to describe the spatial distribution of living standards itself, including inequality \citep{pradhan2025}. The appeal is clear. Household surveys are expensive, infrequent and too small to describe districts or villages, while the predictors used by these models are available everywhere.

This paper is about a simple property of the numbers these maps contain. A machine-learning poverty map reports, for each location or household, a prediction of welfare given observed features: an estimate of the conditional mean $\E[Y\mid X]$. A conditional mean is less dispersed than the variable it predicts. The difference is not noise that averages out. By the law of iterated expectations, true welfare is a mean-preserving spread of its conditional mean, so the distribution of conditional means Lorenz-dominates the distribution of welfare. Every inequality index that respects the Lorenz order is therefore smaller for the conditional mean than for welfare itself, and the same is true of poverty measures that are sensitive to the depth of poverty at a fixed poverty line. An estimated map inherits this property up to estimation noise. When predictions are also shrunk towards the mean, as regularised and ensemble learners typically do, the understatement grows.

The point has a long history in small area estimation. \citet{ell2003} built their poverty mapping method around it: rather than plugging predicted means into poverty and inequality formulas, they simulate household welfare by adding draws of the prediction error, which in effect restores the dispersion that plug-in predictions would remove. \citet{tarozzi2009} and \citet{molina2010} study the conditions under which such simulation is valid. The machine-learning poverty mapping literature has largely moved away from this practice, publishing point predictions and validating them with correlation coefficients or $R^2$. \citet{corral2025} evaluate machine-learning poverty maps against a census benchmark for Mexico and find substantial bias in estimated poverty rates. Studies that map inequality with machine learning acknowledge that predictions may understate dispersion \citep{pradhan2025}, but to our knowledge no paper characterises the bias in \emph{inequality} measures computed from such maps, gives its sign and size, or evaluates corrections for it.

We make four contributions. First, we give a general result: for any Lorenz-consistent inequality index and for the FGT poverty measures with $\alpha \geq 1$, the index computed on the conditional mean, or on any calibrated prediction, is a lower bound for the index computed on welfare (Proposition~\ref{prop:lorenz}). Second, for the mean log deviation and the Theil index the gap is exactly the expected within-cell inequality, with population and income-share weights respectively (Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp}). This makes the connection with the measurement of inequality of opportunity explicit. The inequality of a poverty map is the ex-ante inequality of opportunity of \citet{ferreira2011}, with satellite or phone features in the role of circumstances, and in the population it is a lower bound on total inequality for the same reason that ex-ante inequality of opportunity is a lower bound on inequality. A lognormal example gives a closed form (Proposition~\ref{prop:lognormal}): with a predictive $R^2$ of 0.4 in logs, in the range reported for consumption, the Gini coefficient of the predictions is about two-thirds of the true Gini. Third, we show that linearly recalibrating predictions to undo their shrinkage towards the mean can at best recover the inequality of the oracle conditional mean, never total inequality (Proposition~\ref{prop:shrink}). Fourth, we evaluate three corrections that simulate from an estimated conditional distribution rather than plugging in a conditional mean: homoskedastic residual simulation in the spirit of \citet{ell2003}, quantile regression forests \citep{meinshausen2006}, and a residual bootstrap within bins of the prediction.

We test the theory and the corrections on two real datasets and on simulated data (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The first real dataset is the 2023 American Community Survey (ACS), which we treat as a pseudo-census of 1.3 million households: we draw survey-like samples, train the models, predict welfare for areas outside the sample, and compare the estimated inequality and poverty of each area with its known true value. The second is the satellite-based poverty map of \citet{yeh2020}, which predicts an asset index for 19{,}669 survey clusters in 23 African countries from public Landsat and night-light imagery. The real data show how large the bias is in settings that resemble practice. Simulated data, in which the conditional mean and the truth are known exactly, complement them: they verify the propositions and isolate the mechanisms behind the empirical results.

The bias is large in practice. In the ACS, plug-in maps built from household covariates understate the national Gini by 21\% and the poverty headcount by 33\%. Maps built from area-level features, the analogue of satellite data, understate them by 63\% and almost 100\%, and show no inequality at all within areas; mapped, they paint a country of uniformly equal places. In the African satellite maps, between-cluster inequality is understated by 11--18\% in most country-years. A residual bootstrap within bins of the prediction removes almost all of the bias in national ACS estimates, and in the Gini, the mean log deviation and the FGT measures in the simulations, where linear recalibration at best moves the estimates to the oracle conditional mean and with a noisy learner makes them worse. Two limits remain. Corrections overshoot when residuals are pooled across heterogeneous areas or countries. And no correction recovers the ranking of areas by inequality when the features do not predict within-area dispersion.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} positions the paper relative to three literatures that work with the same object. Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the theoretical results. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the estimators, the two real datasets and the simulation design. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results for the ACS, the African satellite maps and the simulations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Positioning: one object, three literatures}\label{sec:lit}

Three literatures compute statistics on the same object, the distribution of a conditional mean $\E[Y\mid X]$, but they treat the dispersion of $Y$ around it in three different ways (Table~\ref{tab:position}). Small area estimation treats it as part of the target and simulates it. Machine-learning poverty mapping ignores it. Ex-ante inequality of opportunity excludes it on purpose. The contribution of this paper is to place machine-learning poverty maps in this picture: we show what their distribution measures, how far it is from the target, and what it takes to close the gap.

\paragraph{What small area estimation knew, and what it leaves open.}
Poverty mapping in the tradition of \citet{ell2003} combines a survey, in which welfare is observed, with a census, in which only predictors are observed. It fits a model of log consumption in the survey and simulates welfare for every census household by drawing a location effect and a household error. Indices are computed on each simulated census and averaged. The debate that followed concerns how to model the errors: \citet{tarozzi2009} show that the method's standard errors can be badly understated when the model does not hold within small areas, and \citet{molina2010} propose the empirical best predictor under a nested error model. Throughout, it is taken for granted that indices must be computed on simulated welfare, not on predicted means. What this literature does not provide is a general statement of what is lost when the simulation step is skipped, for which indices, and by how much. It also predates feature sets that describe places rather than households, for which the dispersion within an area is not predicted at all. Propositions~\ref{prop:lorenz}--\ref{prop:shrink} supply the first, and the area-context exercises in Sections~\ref{sec:acs} and~\ref{sec:sat} address the second.

\paragraph{What machine-learning poverty maps changed.}
Machine-learning poverty maps changed both the predictors and the output. Features extracted from satellite imagery or mobile phone records \citep{jean2016,blumenstock2015,yeh2020,chi2022} are available everywhere, and the published product is a point prediction for each location. Evaluation focuses on how well predictions track survey values, measured by correlations or $R^2$; the best models reach an $R^2$ between 0.5 and 0.75 for asset indices and less for consumption. Work on the reliability of these maps asks whether their errors are systematic: \citet{aiken2023} document urban--rural differences in accuracy, and \citet{corral2025} document substantial bias in area poverty rates against a census benchmark. Predictions are also used as outcomes in impact evaluations \citep{ratledge2022}, where shrinkage towards the mean attenuates estimated effects, and as inputs to inequality maps \citep{pradhan2025}. Our question is different from accuracy. We ask what the distribution of predictions measures. Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp} gives a precise answer: the share of the variance of log welfare that a map reproduces is its $R^2$, so a map that is accurate by current standards can still omit a large part of inequality. Accuracy and distributional validity are separate properties, and the second has not been evaluated.

\paragraph{What inequality of opportunity offers.}
The ex-ante approach to inequality of opportunity \citep{roemer1998,ferreira2011} computes inequality on the conditional mean of an outcome given circumstances, and \citet{brunori2023} estimate that conditional mean with trees and forests. Within-type dispersion is excluded on purpose, because it is not attributable to circumstances. In the population, inequality computed on observed circumstances is a lower bound for the contribution of all circumstances, because the observed set is incomplete; in finite samples, estimation error can push the estimate either way. Poverty maps compute the same object with geography and satellite features in the role of circumstances, but within-cell dispersion is part of their target. This gives the inequality of a poverty map a precise interpretation, as inequality of opportunity with respect to the map's features (Corollary~\ref{cor:iop}), and explains why it cannot be read as an estimate of inequality.

\paragraph{Position of this paper.}
The paper sits where the three literatures meet. It brings the principle of small area estimation, that inequality must be computed on simulated rather than predicted welfare, to machine-learning poverty maps. It grounds the principle in general results that use the Lorenz order and the decomposition behind inequality of opportunity. And it measures the consequences on real data where the truth is known. We do not propose a new prediction model, and we do not study targeting; our object is the distribution that a map implies.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

Let $Y>0$ denote welfare (consumption, income or a positive transformation of an asset index) in a population, with finite mean $\mu=\E[Y]$, and let $X$ be a vector of predictors. The conditional mean is $m(X)=\E[Y\mid X]$, so that $\E[m(X)]=\mu$. A poverty map reports a prediction $\hat m(X)$, an estimate of $m(X)$, for every unit. The ``naive'' or plug-in estimator of an inequality index $I$ is $I(\hat m(X))$, computed on the distribution of predictions over the target population.

An inequality index $I$ is \emph{Lorenz-consistent} if it is symmetric, scale invariant and satisfies the Pigou--Dalton transfer principle. By \citet{atkinson1970} and \citet{dasgupta1973}, for two distributions with Lorenz curves $L_A$ and $L_B$, $L_A(p)\geq L_B(p)$ for all $p\in[0,1]$ implies $I(A)\leq I(B)$ for every Lorenz-consistent $I$. The Gini coefficient, the generalised entropy family $GE(\theta)$, which includes the mean log deviation ($MLD=GE(0)$) and the Theil index ($T=GE(1)$), and the Atkinson indices are Lorenz-consistent. The Foster--Greer--Thorbecke poverty measures \citep{fgt1984} for a poverty line $z$ are $P_\alpha(Y)=\E[((z-Y)_+/z)^\alpha]$, with the headcount ratio for $\alpha=0$.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:props}

\begin{proposition}[Lorenz dominance of predicted welfare]\label{prop:lorenz}
Let $Y>0$ with $\E[Y]<\infty$, and let $m(X)=\E[Y\mid X]$. Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $L_{m(X)}(p)\geq L_Y(p)$ for all $p\in[0,1]$, and therefore $I(m(X))\leq I(Y)$ for every Lorenz-consistent index $I$, with equality for strictly Schur-convex indices if and only if $Y=m(X)$ almost surely;
\item[(ii)] $P_\alpha(m(X))\leq P_\alpha(Y)$ for every fixed poverty line $z$ and every $\alpha\geq 1$;
\item[(iii)] the sign of $P_0(m(X))-P_0(Y)$ is not determined in general.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $Y=m(X)+\varepsilon$. Since $\E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, also $\E[\varepsilon\mid m(X)]=0$, so $Y$ is a mean-preserving spread of $m(X)$ in the sense of \citet{rothschild1970}. By the conditional Jensen inequality, $\E[\phi(Y)]=\E[\E[\phi(Y)\mid X]]\geq \E[\phi(m(X))]$ for every convex $\phi$; that is, $Y$ dominates $m(X)$ in the convex order. For two distributions with the same mean, convex-order dominance is equivalent to Lorenz dominance \citep{atkinson1970}, which gives the first part of (i). The second part follows from the definition of Lorenz consistency and, for the equality case, from the fact that a strictly Schur-convex index is unchanged by a mean-preserving spread only if the spread is degenerate. For (ii), $\phi_\alpha(y)=((z-y)_+/z)^\alpha$ is convex in $y$ when $\alpha\geq 1$, and the same Jensen argument applies. For (iii), let $m(X)$ take the values $a$ and $b$ with equal probability, and let $Y\mid X$ have a symmetric two-point distribution around $m(X)$ with half-width $h$. If $z\in(a+h,b-h)$, then $P_0(m(X))=P_0(Y)=1/2$. If $z\in(a,a+h)$, then $P_0(m(X))=1/2$ and $P_0(Y)=1/4$, an overstatement. If $z\in(a-h,a)$, then $P_0(m(X))=0$ and $P_0(Y)=1/4$, an understatement.
\end{proof}

The headcount ratio is understated when the poverty line lies below most conditional means, which is the usual case for extreme poverty lines in middle-income settings, and can be overstated when the line lies above many of them. The results apply to any calibrated predictor $\hat m$, that is, any predictor with $\E[Y\mid \hat m]=\hat m$, because $Y$ is then a mean-preserving spread of $\hat m$. An estimated map is not exactly calibrated: estimation noise adds dispersion, and in finite samples a noisy prediction can be more unequal than $m(X)$. The bound is therefore a statement about the population target of the map, and the empirical sections measure how close estimated maps come to it. Part (ii) also requires that the poverty line be fixed. A line defined relative to each distribution, such as a share of its own median, changes with the distribution.

\begin{proposition}[The gap is within-cell inequality]\label{prop:decomp}
Let $MLD(Y\mid X)=\log m(X)-\E[\log Y\mid X]$ and $T(Y\mid X)=\E[(Y/m(X))\log(Y/m(X))\mid X]$ denote the mean log deviation and the Theil index of the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X$, and suppose that $\E|\log Y|<\infty$ and $\E[Y|\log Y|]<\infty$. Then
\begin{align}
MLD(Y) &= MLD(m(X)) + \E\left[MLD(Y\mid X)\right], \label{eq:mld}\\
T(Y) &= T(m(X)) + \E\left[\frac{m(X)}{\mu}\,T(Y\mid X)\right]. \label{eq:theil}
\end{align}
For the variance of logarithms, with $m_\ell(X)=\E[\log Y\mid X]$ and $R^2_\ell$ the population $R^2$ of $\log Y$ on $X$,
\begin{equation}
\Var(\log Y)=\Var(m_\ell(X))+\E[\Var(\log Y\mid X)], \qquad \Var(m_\ell(X))=R^2_\ell\,\Var(\log Y). \label{eq:varlog}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$MLD(Y)=\log\mu-\E[\log Y]$ and $MLD(m(X))=\log\mu-\E[\log m(X)]$, because both distributions have mean $\mu$. The difference is $\E[\log m(X)-\log Y]=\E[\log m(X)-\E[\log Y\mid X]]=\E[MLD(Y\mid X)]$, which is (\ref{eq:mld}). For the Theil index, $T(Y)-T(m(X))=\mu^{-1}\E[Y\log Y-m(X)\log m(X)]$, since the terms in $\log\mu$ cancel. Conditioning on $X$, $\E[Y\log Y\mid X]-m(X)\log m(X)=m(X)\,\E[(Y/m(X))\log(Y/m(X))\mid X]=m(X)\,T(Y\mid X)$, which gives (\ref{eq:theil}). Equation (\ref{eq:varlog}) is the law of total variance.
\end{proof}

Equations (\ref{eq:mld}) and (\ref{eq:theil}) are the between--within decompositions of \citet{bourguignon1979} and \citet{shorrocks1980}, applied to the partition of the population by values of $X$. The plug-in estimator retains the between-cell term and discards the within-cell term. The amount lost depends on how much welfare varies among units that look identical to the model, not on how well the model ranks units. A model can have a high correlation with welfare across clusters and still miss most of the inequality within them.

\begin{corollary}[Poverty-map inequality as ex-ante inequality of opportunity]\label{cor:iop}
If $X$ is interpreted as a vector of circumstances, $I(m(X))$ is the ex-ante parametric or non-parametric measure of inequality of opportunity of \citet{ferreira2011}, and $I(m(X))/I(Y)$ is the share of inequality explained by $X$. Inequality computed on a poverty map is therefore the inequality of opportunity associated with the features the map is built from, and in the population it is a lower bound on total inequality.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Lognormal benchmark]\label{prop:lognormal}
Suppose $\log Y=m_\ell(X)+u$, with $m_\ell(X)\sim N(\nu,R^2_\ell\sigma^2)$ and $u\mid X\sim N(0,(1-R^2_\ell)\sigma^2)$ independent of $X$ (homoskedastic errors). Then $m(X)=\exp\{m_\ell(X)+(1-R^2_\ell)\sigma^2/2\}$ is lognormal with log standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{R^2_\ell}$, and
\begin{equation}
G(Y)=2\Phi\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt 2}\right)-1, \qquad G(m(X))=2\Phi\left(\frac{\sigma\sqrt{R^2_\ell}}{\sqrt 2}\right)-1, \qquad \frac{MLD(m(X))}{MLD(Y)}=R^2_\ell. \label{eq:lognormal}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Gini coefficient of a lognormal distribution with log standard deviation $s$ is $2\Phi(s/\sqrt2)-1$ and its mean log deviation is $s^2/2$ \citep{cowell2011}. Apply these to $s=\sigma$ and $s=\sigma\sqrt{R^2_\ell}$.
\end{proof}

Table~\ref{tab:lognormal} evaluates (\ref{eq:lognormal}) for values of $\sigma$ that correspond to true Gini coefficients between 0.28 and 0.56. With $R^2_\ell=0.4$, the plug-in Gini is 33--36\% below the true Gini; with $R^2_\ell=0.8$, it is still 9--10\% below. The ratio $G(m)/G(Y)$ depends mainly on $R^2_\ell$ and only weakly on the level of inequality.

\begin{proposition}[Shrinkage and mean recalibration]\label{prop:shrink}
Suppose $\hat m(X)=\mu+\lambda\,(m(X)-\mu)$ with $\lambda\in(0,1]$ and $\hat m(X)>0$. Then $G(\hat m(X))=\lambda\,G(m(X))\leq G(Y)$. If predictions are recalibrated by the linear projection of $Y$ on $\hat m(X)$, the recalibrated predictions equal $m(X)$, so that recalibration recovers at most $G(m(X))$ and never $G(Y)$ unless $Y=m(X)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Gini coefficient is half the mean absolute difference divided by the mean. Shrinkage multiplies all pairwise differences by $\lambda$ and leaves the mean unchanged, so it multiplies the Gini by $\lambda$. The inequality $\lambda G(m(X))\leq G(Y)$ follows from $\lambda\leq 1$ and Proposition~\ref{prop:lorenz}. The slope of the linear projection of $Y$ on $\hat m(X)$ is $\Cov(Y,\hat m)/\Var(\hat m)=\lambda\Cov(Y,m)/(\lambda^2\Var(m))=1/\lambda$, because $\Cov(Y,m)=\Var(m)$ when $m$ is the conditional mean; the intercept restores the mean $\mu$. The recalibrated prediction is therefore $\mu+(\hat m-\mu)/\lambda=m(X)$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:shrink} describes the best case for recalibration. In practice the prediction error of a machine-learning model is not pure shrinkage. When $\hat m=m+\eta$ with estimation noise $\eta$, the projection slope is below one, and the mean-squared-error-optimal recalibration contracts the predictions further. The simulation evidence in Section~\ref{sec:mc} shows that this can make linear recalibration worse than doing nothing.

\section{Estimators, data and evaluation design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Estimators}\label{sec:estimators}

The target of a poverty map that reports inequality is $I(F_Y)$, where $F_Y(y)=\int F(y\mid x)\,dF_X(x)$ is the welfare distribution in the target population. Propositions~\ref{prop:lorenz}--\ref{prop:shrink} show that this target depends on the whole conditional distribution $F(y\mid x)$, not only on its mean. All corrections we consider replace the prediction for each target unit by draws $y^*_{ir}$, $r=1,\dots,R$, from an estimate $\hat F(\cdot\mid X_i)$, compute the index on each simulated population, and average over $r$. We compare five estimators. All models are fitted to $\log Y$ in the training sample, and out-of-fold predictions and residuals are obtained by five-fold cross-fitting.

\begin{description}
\item[N (naive plug-in).] $I(\hat m(X))$, with $\hat m(X)=\exp\{\hat m_\ell(X)\}\cdot s$ and $s$ the smearing factor of \citet{duan1983}. This is the conditional mean prediction that poverty maps report.
\item[C1 (mean recalibration).] The naive prediction is replaced by the fitted values of a linear regression of $Y$ on the out-of-fold naive predictions in the training sample. This is the simplest correction for shrinkage towards the mean.
\item[C2 (homoskedastic residual simulation).] $y^*_{ir}=\exp\{\hat m_\ell(X_i)+e^*_{ir}\}$, where $e^*_{ir}$ is drawn with replacement from the pooled out-of-fold log residuals. This is the simulation step of \citet{ell2003} without a separate location effect.
\item[C3 (quantile regression forest).] A quantile regression forest \citep{meinshausen2006} estimates 99 conditional quantiles of $\log Y$; $y^*_{ir}$ is the exponential of the quantile at a randomly drawn level. This allows the conditional distribution to vary with $X$ in a flexible way.
\item[C4 (binned residual bootstrap).] As C2, but residuals are drawn only from training units in the same decile of the out-of-fold prediction. This allows heteroskedasticity along the dimension that matters most for inequality.
\end{description}

When the outcome is an asset index that takes negative values (Section~\ref{sec:data_sat}), the same estimators are applied in levels with additive residuals, and inequality is measured with translation-invariant indices: the absolute Gini (half the mean absolute difference), the standard deviation, the 90--10 percentile range, and the share of units below a fixed cut-off. For the absolute Gini and the standard deviation, Proposition~\ref{prop:lorenz} holds with the Lorenz order replaced by the convex order, because both are Schur-convex and translation invariant, and the absolute Gini satisfies the scaling result of Proposition~\ref{prop:shrink} without the requirement that $\hat m>0$. The percentile range and the share below a cut-off are not monotone in the convex order, and for them the direction of the bias is an empirical question.

\subsection{Two real datasets and a simulation}\label{sec:data_overview}

We evaluate the estimators on three sources of evidence, two of which are real data (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The ACS lets us compare estimates with the true inequality of every area in a real income distribution. The African satellite maps let us do the same for a published, widely used poverty map built from imagery. Simulated data play a supporting role: they are the only setting in which the conditional mean $m(X)$ itself is known, so they are used to verify Propositions~\ref{prop:lorenz}--\ref{prop:shrink} and to separate the effect of using a conditional mean from the effect of estimating it.

\subsection{Real data I: the American Community Survey as a pseudo-census}\label{sec:data_acs}

The first real dataset is the 2023 one-year American Community Survey (ACS) Public Use Microdata Sample as a pseudo-census. In this setting the truth is known in every area. The population is the 1{,}326{,}716 occupied housing units with positive household income in the 50 states and the District of Columbia. Welfare is household income adjusted to 2023 dollars, and all indices use the household weights. The national Gini is 0.474. The poverty line is fixed at 60\% of the weighted national median (USD 47{,}713). The small areas are the 2{,}462 Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs), each with at least 100{,}000 residents.

Each replication mimics a two-stage household survey. We draw 20\% of the PUMAs in every state and then 30 households in each sampled PUMA with probability proportional to the household weight. This gives a training sample of 14{,}790 households in 493 PUMAs. The model is fitted to this sample, and inequality and poverty are then estimated for all households in the 1{,}969 non-sampled PUMAs, about 80\% of the population. The estimates are compared with the true indices of the same households at three levels: each non-sampled PUMA, each state, and the nation.

We use two sets of predictors.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{unit-level} set has 34 household characteristics, including household size and composition, tenure, rooms, vehicles, the number of workers, and the age, sex, education, race, employment and hours of the household head. It mimics a census-based poverty map in which survey and census share household covariates.
\item The \emph{area-context} set has 13 PUMA averages of census variables, such as the share of owners, mean rooms and vehicles, the share of heads with a degree, and the share employed. Every household in a PUMA has the same values. This mimics satellite and geospatial poverty maps, whose features describe places rather than households.
\end{itemize}

The out-of-fold $R^2$ of log income is 0.50 with unit-level predictors and 0.04 with area-context predictors. We report results for gradient boosting with ten replications and $R=10$ simulation draws. The quantile forest uses 19 quantile levels, from 0.05 to 0.95, to keep computation feasible for a million target households. Results for OLS are reported in the replication package. The plug-in and simulation results are similar, but OLS has a calibration slope of 0.64, so C1 does even worse (national Gini bias $-44\%$).

\subsection{Real data II: satellite poverty maps in Africa}\label{sec:data_sat}

The second real dataset is a widely used satellite poverty map. \citet{yeh2020} predict the cluster mean of an asset-wealth index for 19{,}669 clusters of the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) in 23 African countries, observed between 2009 and 2016. The predictors are publicly available Landsat imagery and night lights. We use their public replication data: the wealth index of each cluster, and per-cluster histograms of the Landsat bands and night lights, which give 636 features. We do not use their convolutional network; our ridge and gradient boosting models reach out-of-fold $R^2$ values of 0.5 to 0.65 within country-years. These are in the range reported for this type of model.

Two features of this setting differ from the ACS. First, the unit is a cluster, not a household, so we measure \emph{between-cluster} inequality within each of the 43 country-year surveys. Second, the asset index is centred and takes negative values. We therefore use the translation-invariant measures of Section~\ref{sec:estimators}: the absolute Gini, the standard deviation, the 90--10 range, and the share of clusters below the pooled 40th percentile of wealth. We consider two validation schemes. \emph{In-country} predictions come from a model pooled across all countries, with the clusters of each country divided into five spatial blocks; the predictions for a block come from a model trained on the other four blocks of every country. \emph{Out-of-country} predictions come from models trained on the other 22 countries, which is how such maps are used for countries without recent surveys. All estimators use $R=200$ simulation draws. For computational reasons, C1, C2 and C4 use calibration data from the outer cross-fitting folds rather than nested folds, which can make the residual pools slightly optimistic.

\subsection{Simulated data}\label{sec:mc_design}

We draw ten correlated standard normal predictors with pairwise correlation 0.3 and generate
\begin{equation*}
\log Y = 10 + f(X) + s(X)\,\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim N(0,1),
\end{equation*}
where $f$ combines linear terms with an interaction, a sine term and a quadratic term, scaled so that $\Var(f(X))=R^2_\ell\,\sigma^2$ with $\sigma^2=0.64$ (a true Gini of about 0.43). In the homoskedastic design $s(X)$ is constant. In the heteroskedastic design $s(X)\propto\exp(0.3X_5)$, normalised so that $\E[s(X)^2]=(1-R^2_\ell)\sigma^2$. The grid covers $R^2_\ell\in\{0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8\}$, training samples of 2{,}000 and 10{,}000 observations, and four learners: ordinary least squares and the lasso on $\log Y$, a random forest and gradient boosting (LightGBM). Each replication draws a new training sample and a new target population of 100{,}000 units; the poverty line is 60\% of the true median. We run eight replications per cell, with $R=50$ simulation draws for C2--C4. Because the data-generating process is known, we can also compute the oracle conditional mean $m(X)$.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We report the results for the two real datasets first and then use the simulations to explain them.

\subsection{American Community Survey}\label{sec:acs}

Figure~\ref{fig:acsmap} maps the problem. It shows the Gini coefficient of every non-sampled PUMA in one replication with household predictors. In the true map (panel a), the 10th and 90th percentiles of PUMA Ginis are 0.39 and 0.49. The highest state averages are in the District of Columbia, Louisiana, New Mexico and Mississippi, and the lowest are in Utah and Maryland. The plug-in map (panel b) is uniformly paler: its interdecile range is 0.32 to 0.39, so the most unequal PUMA in the plug-in map is about as unequal as a typical PUMA in reality. The plug-in map preserves part of the ranking (Spearman correlation 0.57), but it shifts the whole map downwards. The corrected map (panel c) restores the level (interdecile range 0.44 to 0.49) but compresses the differences between areas: its cross-PUMA standard deviation is 0.020, against 0.038 in the truth. The rest of this subsection quantifies these patterns over ten replications.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig1.pdf}
\caption{Gini coefficient of household income by Public Use Microdata Area (PUMA), contiguous United States, one replication of the ACS design: (a) true value, (b) plug-in map from gradient boosting with household predictors, (c) binned residual bootstrap (C4) from the same model. Grey areas are PUMAs in the training sample}\label{fig:acsmap}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:acs} reports the relative bias. With unit-level predictors, the plug-in map understates the national Gini by 21\%, the MLD by 42\% and the headcount by 33\%. PUMA-level indices are understated by similar amounts, and the plug-in PUMA Gini is below its true value in all but 0.03\% of PUMA-replications. Mean recalibration makes things worse. The calibration slope of gradient boosting is 0.85, so C1 contracts the predictions and raises the Gini bias to 28\%.

The binned residual bootstrap (C4) is almost exactly unbiased at the national level for all three indices. At the PUMA level it overstates the Gini by 6\% and the headcount by 5\%. This is because residual dispersion is pooled across areas within a prediction decile, which assigns national within-decile dispersion to more homogeneous places. Homoskedastic simulation (C2) overstates inequality by 8 to 14\% nationally and by more within PUMAs. The 19-point quantile forest (C3) has the smallest PUMA-level error (relative RMSE of 6.7\%) but understates national inequality, because truncating at the 5th and 95th percentiles thins the tails.

The area-context regime is the relevant benchmark for geospatial maps, and here the plug-in estimates are uninformative about inequality. Because every household in a PUMA receives the same prediction, the within-PUMA Gini, MLD and headcount of the plug-in map are exactly zero, whatever the true values (Fig.~\ref{fig:acs}b). National inequality is not zero, but it is only between-PUMA inequality in predicted means. The plug-in national Gini is 0.17 against a truth of 0.47, and the plug-in headcount is 0.1\% against 30\%. These are the magnitudes that Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp} implies when almost all inequality lies within areas.

The simulation estimators recover the level of inequality even with an $R^2$ of 0.04. C4 is within 2\% of the national Gini and within 1\% of the national headcount. The same exercise also shows a limit of the corrections. With uninformative predictors, C2 and C4 assign almost the same residual distribution to every PUMA. They therefore get the average PUMA Gini right, but their rank correlation with the true PUMA Ginis is close to zero (0.01 and 0.06). The quantile forest lets the conditional distribution depend on the context variables and ranks PUMAs better ($\rho=0.41$), at the cost of a downward level bias. A map can only rank areas by inequality if its features predict the within-area dispersion of welfare, not just its mean.

Point corrections do not deliver valid inference by themselves. In five of the ten replications we computed 90\% percentile intervals from a PUMA-cluster bootstrap of the training sample, with 30 bootstrap fits per replication. Intervals around the plug-in estimates never cover the truth, at either the national or the state level, because they are centred on a biased estimate. Intervals around C4 cover the national Gini and MLD in most replications with unit-level predictors (four and five out of five), but only in two of five with area-context predictors. At the state level, however, coverage is only 25\% with unit-level predictors and 49\% with area-context predictors, far below the nominal 90\%. The bootstrap captures the uncertainty of the fitted model. It does not capture the error that comes from pooling residual distributions across areas, and that error dominates for states. Valid area-level inference therefore requires a model for area-specific dispersion, for example a random effect in the conditional variance. We leave this to future work; with only five replications, the coverage figures are indicative.

\subsection{Satellite poverty maps in Africa}\label{sec:sat}

Panel (b) shows, for each country, the relative bias of the plug-in absolute Gini, averaged over survey years, for gradient boosting with in-country validation. Table~\ref{tab:sat} reports the results and Fig.~\ref{fig:sat} plots estimated against true absolute Gini by country-year. The plug-in map understates between-cluster inequality in most surveys. With gradient boosting, the absolute Gini of the predictions is on average 11\% below the truth, both in-country and out-of-country, and below the truth in 84--88\% of country-years. With ridge regression the understatement is 17--18\%. The median ratio of predicted to true absolute Gini is 0.89 for boosting and 0.80 for ridge. The median ratio of the standard deviations of predicted and true wealth is 0.90 for boosting and 0.85 for ridge, almost the same as the Gini ratios. This is what Proposition~\ref{prop:shrink} implies when the dispersion of the prediction is a scaled version of the dispersion of the target. Mean recalibration again moves the estimates further from the truth, by 1 to 5 percentage points. The share of clusters below the poverty cut-off is misestimated by 7 to 19 percentage points on average.

Unlike in the national ACS estimates, the corrections here overshoot. C2, C3 and C4 overstate the absolute Gini by 10 to 20\% and the standard deviation by 8 to 15\%, and the overstatement is larger out of country. The likely reason is the one seen in the ACS at the PUMA level. All residuals come from a model pooled across 23 countries, so the residual distribution assigned to each cluster includes cross-country differences in how well wealth is predicted. That dispersion is larger than the within-country-year residual dispersion that the target index needs. The corrections also do not improve the ranking of countries by inequality: the Spearman correlation between estimated and true absolute Gini is 0.75 to 0.83 in-country and lower out of country, whatever the estimator. In this application the plug-in and the corrected estimates bracket the truth. The plug-in map is on average below the truth, as the theory predicts, and estimators that pool residuals across very different populations give an upper bound.

\subsection{Simulation evidence on the mechanisms}\label{sec:mc}\label{sec:mc_results}

Simulated data complement the two real datasets in one respect: the conditional mean $m(X)$ is known, so the bias of a perfect map (the oracle) can be separated from the bias due to estimation. The simulations confirm the propositions. In all 128 cell-replications the oracle index is below the true index for the Gini, MLD, Theil, Atkinson(1), FGT1 and FGT2. The gap between the true MLD and the oracle MLD equals the average within-cell MLD to within sampling error (0.1602 against 0.1600 on average), as Eq.~(\ref{eq:mld}) requires. In the lognormal benchmark the closed forms in Eq.~(\ref{eq:lognormal}) match Monte Carlo values to within 0.001.

Table~\ref{tab:mc} reports the relative bias of each estimator for gradient boosting with 10{,}000 training observations in the heteroskedastic design, and Fig.~\ref{fig:mc} plots the bias of the Gini and the headcount against $R^2_\ell$. Three findings stand out.

First, the plug-in bias is large and close to the oracle bias. At $R^2_\ell=0.4$ the plug-in Gini is 33\% below the truth, the MLD 57\% below and the headcount 75\% below; at $R^2_\ell=0.8$ the Gini is still 11\% too low. For gradient boosting almost all of this bias is already present in the oracle (Gini $-30\%$ at $R^2_\ell=0.4$): the problem is the use of a conditional mean, not the quality of the learner. The linear learners are misspecified for the nonlinear $f$, and their plug-in bias is larger ($-45\%$ at $R^2_\ell=0.4$). The headcount is strongly understated because, with a poverty line at 60\% of the median, few conditional means fall below the line.

Second, mean recalibration (C1) does not fix the problem and can make it worse. For OLS, the lasso and the random forest the slope of the outcome on the out-of-fold prediction exceeds one, and recalibration stretches the predictions. The corrected Gini then moves close to the oracle value ($-34\%$ for OLS at $R^2_\ell=0.4$, against $-30\%$ for the oracle) but no further, as Proposition~\ref{prop:shrink} predicts. For gradient boosting at $R^2_\ell\leq 0.4$ the slope is below one (0.79 at $R^2_\ell=0.2$ and 0.90 at $R^2_\ell=0.4$), because the predictions contain estimation noise rather than only shrinkage. Recalibration then contracts the predictions further and the Gini bias rises from $-33\%$ to $-38\%$. At higher $R^2_\ell$, recalibration of the boosting predictions moves them towards the oracle.

Third, the simulation estimators remove most of the bias. The binned residual bootstrap (C4) has a Gini bias below 1\% in absolute value and an MLD bias of at most 2\% at every $R^2_\ell$, and it is nearly unbiased for the three FGT measures. It understates the Theil index, which is more sensitive to the top of the distribution, by 3.5 to 4.9\% for $R^2_\ell\geq 0.4$. With gradient boosting, homoskedastic residual simulation (C2) overstates inequality and especially the depth of poverty when the predictive $R^2$ is low, because pooled residuals are too dispersed for the better-predicted part of the distribution; with the linear learners it understates the Gini by about 4\%. The quantile regression forest (C3) understates inequality by a few per cent, mainly because its predicted quantiles are smoothed within leaves and truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles, which thins the tails. The binned bootstrap is also among the least biased estimators in the homoskedastic design, with 2{,}000 training observations and with the other learners; in a few cells with low $R^2_\ell$, C2 is marginally closer.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A machine-learning poverty map reports conditional means. Conditional means are less unequal than the welfare they predict, so inequality and poverty-depth measures computed from a map are biased downwards, up to estimation noise. For the mean log deviation and the Theil index, the bias equals expected within-cell inequality. The inequality of a map is better read as inequality explained by its features, an ex-ante inequality of opportunity with geography as the circumstance, than as an estimate of inequality.

The size of the bias depends on what the features can predict. With household covariates from the ACS, national inequality is understated by about a fifth. With area-level features, which describe places rather than households, within-area inequality in the map is zero by construction and the national Gini is understated by almost two-thirds. Between-cluster inequality in satellite maps of African countries is understated by 11 to 18\%. Simulations with a known truth show that most of this bias is already present in a perfect conditional-mean map: with a predictive $R^2$ of 0.4 in logs, the oracle Gini is 30\% too low. Linear recalibration of predictions for shrinkage can at best recover the inequality of the conditional mean, and with noisy learners it widens the gap.

Simulating welfare from an estimated conditional distribution, as small area estimation has long done, removes most of the level bias when the residual distribution is estimated on data that resemble the target population. A residual bootstrap within bins of the prediction does well nationally in the pseudo-census of United States incomes and in simulations. Two caveats apply. First, when residuals are pooled across heterogeneous populations, the corrections overshoot, as they do within PUMAs in the ACS and across countries in the satellite application. Second, simulation recovers the level of inequality but not its spatial pattern when the features do not predict within-area dispersion, and bootstrap intervals that ignore this source of error undercover at the state level.

The practical implications are as follows. Plug-in poverty maps should not be used to compare inequality across places or over time, or to report inequality levels, without a distributional correction. When they are used, the plug-in value should be reported as a lower bound together with the out-of-sample $R^2$, which by Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp} indicates how much inequality is missing. Producers of maps can make correction possible by publishing estimated conditional distributions, or at least residual variances by area, rather than point predictions.

The paper has limitations. Our design-based validation uses United States income data, because no public census of household consumption with known small-area truth exists for a low-income country. Our satellite application measures between-cluster inequality of an asset index, not household consumption inequality. Validation against census microdata in low- and middle-income countries, along the lines of \citet{corral2025}, and estimators that model area-specific residual dispersion are natural next steps.
